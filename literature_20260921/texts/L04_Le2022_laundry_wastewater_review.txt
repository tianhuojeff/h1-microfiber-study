Abstract  
 
Data corroborated in this study highlights laundry wastewater as a primary source of 
microfibers (MFs) in the aquatic environment. MFs can negatively impact the aquatic 
ecosystem via five possible pathways, namely, acting as carriers of other contaminats, 
physical damage to digestive systems of aquatic organisms, blocking the digestive tract, 
releasing toxic chemicals, and harbouring invasive and noxious plankton and bacteria. This 
review shows that small devices to capture MFs during household laundry activities are 
simple to use and affordable at household level in developed countries. However, these low 
cost and small devices are unrealiable and can only achieve up to 40% MF removal 
efficiency. In line filtration devices can achieve higher removal efficiency under well 
maintained condition but their performance is still limited compared to over 98% MF 
removal by large scale centralized wastewater treatment. These results infers that effort to 
increase sanitation coverage to ensure adequate wastewater treatment prior to environmental 
discharge is likely to be more cost effective than those small devices for capturing MFs. This 
review also shows that natural fabrics would entail significantly less environmental 
consequences than synthetic materials. Contribution from the fashion industry to increase the 
share of natural frabics in the current textile market can also reduce the loading of plastic 
MFs in the environment. 
 
Keywords: Laundry activities, Microplastic fibers, Natural fabrics, Washing machines, 
Wastewater treatment.

1. Introduction 
Microfibers (MFs) pollution is an emerging environmental problem of a global scale. 
Specific and widespread evidence has emerged in recent years to show a range of harmful 
effects from MFs on aquatic habitats and even potential impacts on human health (Bilal et al., 
2020). Scientists estimated that millions of tonnes of MFs enter the aquatic environment each 
year from the textile industry and laundry household (Napper and Thompson, 2016; Carney 
Almroth et al., 2018). MFs are particularly mobile, thus, even after landfilling, they can be 
transferred to leachate and eventually find their way to the aquatic environment (Silva et 
al.,2021). Synthetic MFs such as polyester, acrylic, and nylon are non-biodegradable, thus, 
once entered the aquatic environment, they are present everywhere from shorelines (Barrows 
et al., 2018) to the ocean. MFs have been detected in the digestive system of aquatic 
organisms, then they accumulate in the food chain, especially in fish and shellfish (Waring et 
al., 2018; Qiao et al., 2019; Weis, 2020; Jong et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). MFs can also 
release toxic chemicals such as plasticizers that have been added to textiles during 
manufacturing to modify their physical properties (Sridharan et al., 2022). MFs can act as 
transport vectors to carry these chemicals to affected organisms in the environment. Humans 
are also indirectly exposed to microfibers through the food and drinking water.   
Recent demand and consumption of synthetic fabrics have further exacerbated the problem of 
MFs in the environment. Indeed, the demand for synthetic fabrics has grown exponentially 
over the last decade. Between 2010 to 2019, the compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 
the global synthetic fabric market was 4%. It is forecasted to grow to over 7%/yr over the 
next decade (Zhang and Sesto, 2019). It is also estimated that Asia will experience a faster 
CAGR growth due to significant economic development in the region.  
Synthetic fabrics currently account for 63% of the worldwide fabric market, the majority of 
which is polyester. Polyester is the predominant source (52%) of synthetic fabric, followed 

 
 
3 
 
by polyamide (5%), and other synthetic  (6%) (Anonymous, 2020). Polyester fabric has been 
widely used for multipurpose (e.g., plastic packaging and fashion) due to its mechanical 
strength, aesthetic appearance, lightweight, and low cost.  Polyester accounted for more than 
half of global MF textile in 2018 (around 55 million tonnes) from the textile industry (Freitas 
et al., 2017). 
Synthetic fabrics such as nylon, polyester, spandex, and acrylic were initially produced as 
low-cost alternatives to natural fabrics as they can be synthezised from petroleum-based 
chemicals (Franco et al., 2020). For example, acrylic is used to imitate wool due to its good 
heat insulation properties (Ahmad et al., 2020). Artificial fur and fleece made from acrylic 
fibers significantly reduce the materials cost for cloth manufacturing. Early synthetic fabrics 
had inferior texture properties (e.g., look and feel) compared to natural fabrics. Over time, 
progress in materials science and engineering has significantly improved the texture 
properties of synthetic fabrics, making them similar and, in some cases, even better than 
natural materials (Egan et al., 2022). As a result, synthetic fabrics have gradually displaced 
natural fabrics to dominate the textile industry. 
As the potentially significant and irreversible impact of the presence of MFs on the 
environment has become clearer recent years, dedicated scientific studies and engineering 
measures have also emerged to limit their release to the natural environment. The release of 
MFs to the natural environment is closely linked to laundry activities and the textile industry 
through wastewater discharge (Henry et al., 2019).  
This work aims to critically examine laundry wastewater and from the textile industry to seek 
a holistic solution to the issue of MFs in the natural environment. Potential effects of MFs on 
the ecosystem are highlighted to provide a context to this review. Data corroborated from the 
literature are then analysed to delineate key factors (e.g. temperature, fabric types, detergents,

and washing methods) influencing the release of MFs to wastewater during laudry activies. 
Current and future approached to reduce or eliminate the release of MFs to the natural 
environment are then explored and discussed. 
2. Microfibers in laundry wastewater 
2.1. Microfibers 
As a subset of microplastics, MFs are small plastic particles of less than 5 mm in size 
(Lebreton et al., 2018; Bui et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2020). Some MFs from textiles are longer 
than 5 mm, but their diameter fits into microplastics categories. In addition, after entering the 
environment, these long plastic fibers can also be broken down into segments less than 5 mm 
in length (De Falco et al., 2020). MFs are essentially primary microplastics released into the 
environment along the supply chain of the fashion industry from textile manufracturing, 
fashion production, use and laundry of textile products, and textile waste disposal (Henry et 
al., 2019).  
Previous studies (Booth et al., 2017; Uddin et al., 2020) have confirmed significant annual 
input of microplastics to the environment, mainly from poorly managed waste and 
wastewater disposal. Due to their highly mobile characteristics, in the aquatic environment, 
MFs are arguably the most important subgroup of microplastics (Kazmiruk et al., 2018; Yang 
et al., 2019). 
Human activities are the main source of MFs in the aquatic environment (Belzagui et al., 
2019). Annual release of MFs to the environment via wastewater discharge varies from 
country to country ranging from hundreds to tens of thousands tonnes/yr (Table 1). China 
accounts for more than 40% (about 70,000 tonnes) of the global MFs emissions to the aquatic 
environment, mostly through untreated wastewater discharge (Gavigan et al., 2020). North

America also contributes approximately 880 tonnes/yr (Vassilenko et al., 2019). At the other 
end of the spectrum, Finland contributes 150 tonnes/yr of MFs to the environment.

of MFs to the environment (Yang et al., 2019).
2.2. Environmental effects of MFs 1 
In the aquatic environment, MFs can cause several environmental consequences (Fig. 1). 2 
They can act as carriers of harmful contaminants, algae species and pathogens (Kwon et al., 3 
2017; Amelia et al., 2021; Allouzi et al., 2021) from one place to another in the aquatic 4 
environment.  Due to the high absorptive capacity for hydrophobic organic chemicals and a 5 
large surface area to the volume ratio, co-contaminants could attach to MFs (Amelia et al., 6 
2021) to produce toxic aggregates. In this case, MFs become a vector or vehicle for 7 
transferring toxic co-contaminants to organisms via a direct food uptake way (Fig. 1). Heavy 8 
metals (e.g., Cd and Pb) and toxic organics such as Chlordecone, bisphenol A, phthalates, and 9 
polybrominated diphenyl ethers (Scopetani et al., 2018; Weis, 2020; Yan et al., 2020; 10 
Cormier et al., 2021) have been detected on the surface of MFs in aquatic environment. 11 

 
 
6 
 
Previous studies indicated that toxic chemicals could sorb to MPs, particularly the surface of 12 
aging MPs, and could negatively impact the growth of some aquatic organisms (Cormier et 13 
al., 2021). For example, tributyltin is a toxic organic chemical and could accumulate in the 14 
food chain at a low concentration (Leung et al., 2001). The reported LC50 concentration of 15 
tributyltin could range from 0.015 ppb for Tigriopus japonicas (Kwok and Leung, 2005) to 16 
36 ppb for Tisbe biminiensis (Costa et al., 2014). Besides, the plastic surface could also be a 17 
reservoir of pathogenic bacteria (e.g., Aeromonas salmonicida) through biofilm (Viršek et al., 18 
2017; Rotjan et al., 2019; Sooriyakumar et al., 2022). Due to the protection of the biofilm, 19 
some microorganisms living in the biofilm are tolerant to toxic organic chemicals, such as 20 
antibiotics and antimicrobial agents, as well as heavy metals. Then, the biofilm becomes 21 
thicker and separates from the microplastic surface, contributing to the transfer of those toxic 22 
substances from one place to another in the aquatic environment (Bolan et al ., 2020; 23 
Sooriyakumar et al., 2022). 24 
Aquatic organisms digest MPs through their gills or by confusion with their prey (Savoca et 25 
al., 2019; Zantis et al., 2021). Among sub-groups of MPs, MFs was the most prevalent in the 26 
digestive system of an aquatic animal at about 60%, followed by plastic fragment (26%), 27 
films and pellets (14%) (Issac and Kandasubramanian, 2021). MFs could cause more severe 28 
damage to digestive system than microbeads and large MP fragments because of their high 29 
mobility. Once entered the digestive system, MFs can form entanglement causing intestinal 30 
blockage and other damages (Qiao et al., 2019; Weis, 2020) (Fig.1). In addition, MFs could 31 
decrease the mucus volume in the gut, leading to the decreasing working capacity immune 32 
function of the intestinal epithelium and intestinal epithelial damage (Qiao et al., 2019).  33 
Inside the animal body, MPs can release co-contaminants, plasticizers, and other chemicals 34 
that can bioaccumulate in important organs, especially liver (Fig. 1). Barboza et al. (2018) 35 
observed that mercury concentration in the liver was higher than that in the gills of D. labrax 36 

 
 
7 
 
juveniles. The level of Hg in gills ranged from 1.6 to 5.2 μg/g, whereas in the liver, they 37 
ranged from 3 μg/g to 8 μg/g. The toxic element Cd, absorbed into MPs, was mainly detected 38 
in the liver (Lu et al., 2018). Ag accumulated in the intestinal mucous, mucosal epithelium, 39 
muscle layer, and serosa (Khan et al., 2017). The accumulation of toxins in the liver and 40 
tissues could seriously impact organisms, such as DNA damage, oxidative stress, 41 
neurotoxicity, and physiological effects. 42 
In the environment, MPs can harbor and encourage the proliferation of invasive 43 
phytoplankton and noxious species (Fig. 1), resulting in biodiversity loss and posing a threat 44 
to the aquatic ecosystem (Shahul Hamid et al., 2018; Thushari and Senevirathna, 2020). 45 
Some alien species often used the surface of floating MPs in the aquatic environment as a 46 
habitat for growth. About 2.5x1011 particles of MPs buoyant in the Mediterranean Sea 47 
(Werner et al., 2016) were potential carriers of the invasive species based on MPs' biofouling 48 
and persistent properties.  Casabianca et al. (2019) provided evidence that toxic microalgal 49 
species (e.g., Bacillariophyceae and Dinophyceae) could survive on the surface of floating 50 
MPs in high amounts ranging from 61-100 cells/mg in the Mediterranean Sea. With the 51 
change of ocean currents and sea wind, MPs - a toxic vector could be dispersed to new distant 52 
aquatic areas, subsequently increasing the toxin in marine environments due to the 53 
proliferation of invasive and noxious organisms.  54

2.3. Microfibers from laundry activities 58 
2.2.1 Microfibers from laundry activities 59 
Laundry activities have been identified as the most significant source of MFs in the 60 
environment (Hernandez et al., 2017; De Falco et al., 2019). MFs can enter the environment 61 
throughout the supply chain of the fashion industry from textile fabrication, clothing 62 
manufacturing to including domestic laundry activities (Acharya et al., 2021; Ross et al., 63 

 
 
8 
 
2021). The release of MFs from textile fabrication and clothing manufacturing can be readily 64 
addressed via centralized wastewater treatment (Bui et al., 2020; Pedrotti et al., 2020). On the 65 
other hand, the dispersed nature of domestic laundry activities makes them especially 66 
difficult to control. 67 
2.2.2 Factors influencing the release of microfibers from laundry activities 68 
The release of MFs from the laundry depends on several factors, such as textile 69 
characteristics (e.g., fabric age and type), detergent properties and strength, washing 70 
temperature, and abrasion during washing (Fig. 2).  New clothes shed more MFs during 71 
washing due to residues from the fabric production process (Belzagui et al., 2019; De Falco 72 
et al., 2019). In addition, the first few washing cycles also release significantly more MFs 73 
than later cycles when the release of MFs have reached a steady state. The release of 74 
polyester MFs was only 1/3 in the second wash compared to the first time of new clothes, and 75 
the number of shedding MFs dropped by 1/10 on the fifth wash for all polyester and cotton 76 
MFs (Sillanpää and Sainio, 2017). MFs shedding also depends on fabric materials. MFs 77 
shedding from polyester fleece fabric was twice as much as acryl fabric (Kärkkäinen and 78 
Sillanpää, 2021). 79 
The use of detergent, both in liquid and powder form, increased MFs release (Fig. 2). 80 
Detergent chemically removed dirt and debris trapped in fabrics and washed them away by 81 
generating a chemical reaction with water and producing alkalinity. However, a high pH 82 
condition (e.g., above pH 9.0 for liquid) could induce chemical damage on polyester fabrics 83 
through hydrolysis (Bishop, 1995) and thus, washing with detergents resulting in the higher 84 
release of MFs by 1.2-6.0 folds compared to without detergents (Yang et al., 2019). It is also 85 
suggested that powder detergents may result in more MFs release than liquid detergents 86 
(Issac and Kandasubramanian, 2021; Periyasamy 2021). This is because powder detergents 87 
contain insoluble particles to increase abrasion and cause friction with the fabrics.  88 

 
 
9 
 
The increase in washing temperature also resulted in an increase in MFs release (Yang et al., 89 
2019; Zambrano et al., 2021) (Fig. 2). Hot water washing at 60oC released more polyester 90 
MFs (16x103 of MFs) than at lower washing temperature (~30oC) (Yang et al., 2019). Indeed, 91 
washing clothes at a lower temperature could reduce the number of released MFs by up to 92 
50% (Cotton et al., 2020). Washing at a high temperature promotes the expansion of yarns, 93 
the bonds between the fibers become loose, creating more free spaces in the fabric structure  94 
for the mobilization of the broken MFs, particularly under high alkalinity conditions 95 
(Nishimurat and Sarko, 1987). Therefore, synthetic fabric textiles should be washed at low 96 
temperatures such as 30°C, as higher temperatures promote MFs release. 97

Abrasion during laundry can contribute more to fabric damage than wear and tear, thus, 100 
releasing more MFs (Fig. 2). The surface abrasion of the fabric increases mechanical stress, 101 
breaks MFs structure, and releases more MFs during laundry activity (Jönsson et al., 2018). 102 
Besides, the abrasion depended on several factors, such as the spin-dry rate of the washing 103 
machine, the use of detergent or surfactants to remove dirt on clothes, and the loading drum. 104 
During dry spinning, the fabric can be subject to abrasion and centrifugal force, causing a 105 
further release of MFs to laundry wastewater (Sillanpää and Sainio, 2017). In addition, the 106 
high alkalinity of detergents could also produce MFs fuzz on the fabric surface during the 107 
laundry. Then, the abrasion process inside the washing machine immediately broke the fuzz, 108 
releasing MFs to the aquatic environment. According to Cai et al. (2021), the number of MFs 109 
detached from fleece after abrasion experiments were about 3,000 MFs/gram of textile fabric, 110 
which were 5-folds higher than that of non-abrasive condition.  111

3.1. Traps and filters 113 
There have been several commercial products or small devices for capturing MFs during 114 
laundry activities. These products can be placed either directly inside the washing drum or 115 
fitted to washing machines to remove MFs from laundry wastewater. Examples of the former 116 
include laundry bags (e.g. Guppyfriend) and capturing balls (e.g. Cora). The latter are in-line 117 
filters such as Lint LUV-R and Filtrol™  that can be fitted directly to laundry equipment 118

3.2. Wastewater treatment 142 
3.2.1 The removal of microplastics by municipal wastewater treatment 143 
In most wealthy countries, laundry water is released to the sewer for treatment at a 144 
centralized wastewater treatment plant (WWTPs). WWTPs can achieve complete or near 145 
complete removal of MFs from household laundry activities through several and often 146 
redundant treatment stages (Sun et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2021). 147 
As denoted in Fig. 3, primary treatment accounts for the initial 40-65% removal of the MFs 148 
(Sun et al., 2019). Primary treatment (which is the first treatment stage), typically includes 149 
screening, grit and grease removal, and primary settlement. MFs are usually long, light, and 150 
easy to float on the water, so they are effectively captured by screening and floatation for oil 151 
and grease removal (Talvitie et al., 2017). Secondary treatment can add about 20-60% MF 152 
removal on top of the efficiency of primary treatment. In this stage, small and suspended MFs 153 
that have escaped primary treatment can be captured by adsorption to activated sludge flocs 154 
and then removed by the secondary clarification tank. Tertiary treatment processes such as 155 
sand filtration and microfiltration can then remove any remaining MFs in the water by size 156 
exclusion prior to effluent discharge. In this phase, an alternative technique - membrane 157 
bioreactor could reach an excellent removal performance (90–99%) compared to others, e.g., 158 
disc filter (80–98%), granular filter (80–98%), dissolved air flotation (60–85%) (Talvitie et 159 

 
 
12 
 
al., 2017; Enfrin et al., 2020). The three stages offer complementary and redundant treatment 160 
capability to ensure a high MF removal rate.  161 
It is noteworthy that not all WWTPs include all three treatment stages. Tertiary treatment is 162 
only used when the effluent is intended for water reuse or discharged to a sensitive 163 
environment (Bui et al., 2020). Even without tertiary treatment, WWTPs can provide an 164 
effective and reliable performance for removing MFs from wastewater.    165

4. Addressing the root cause 169 
4.1. Synthetic fabrics 170 
Fabrics used in the fashion industry can be of natural or synthetic origin. Natural fabrics are 171 
from plant-based (e.g., cotton, linen, and jute) or animal-based (e.g., wool and silk) fibers 172 
(Syduzzaman et al., 2020). Silk is naturally obtained from an insect and produced from the 173 
substance used to form a cocoon. Wool is from sheep or other furry animals. It is a highly 174 
desirable product due to its capacity to hold heat and natural appearance. Cotton is the most 175 
common form of plant-based fibers that can be obtained from cotton seeds. Linen is a strong 176 
and lightweight fabric made from fibers of the flax plant. Jute fibers are from the skin of the 177 
jute plant. It is used to make several fabrics such as hessian cloth, sacking, scrim, carpet 178 
backing cloth, and canvas. Jute fabrics have exceptionally high strength, but they have a 179 
rough texture. Thus, their applications in the fashion industry are limited. Aside from the 180 
commonly used natural fibers (e.g., cotton and silk), regenerated cellulosic fibers (e.g., 181 
viscose, rayon) have also become popular. They are made from dissolved wood pulp. 182 
Regenerated cellulosic fibers have physical and chemical properties similar to cotton, such as 183 
water absorbency and close to skin comfort. Primary applications for regenerated cellulosic 184 

 
 
13 
 
fibers are in the hygiene and wipes sectors (Ahmad et al., 2014; Muthu, 2017; Subash and 185 
Muthiah, 2021).  186

The remaining is from natural fibers, mostly cotton (25%) and regenerated cellulosic fabrics 188 
(7%) with a small contribution from silk and wool. As plant-based materials, regenerated 189 
cellulosic fabrics can make the fashion industry more sustainable since the raw materials are 190 
renewable and the final products can be naturally assimilated into the environment at the end 191 
of their service life (Anonymous, 2020). 192 
Despite significant improvement in texture properties, synthetic fabrics have an inherent 193 
problem. They are extremely persistent in the environment. The degradation of synthetic 194 
fabrics could be governed by polymer types, density, molecular size, additives, biological 195 
activity, and environmental conditions. For example, polyester (the most common synthetic 196 
fabric) is essentially polyethylene terephthalate or PET. Like a PET water bottle, it takes 197 
more than 200 years to fully biodegrade polyester in the natural environment (Fig. 4). Royer 198 
et al. (2021) simulated marine conditions to compare the biodegradability of regenerated 199 
cellulosic and polyester fibers. After 90 days, Royer et al. (2021) observed some or complete 200 
degradation of regenerated cellulosic fibers. By contrast, polyester fibers did not show any 201 
evidence of biodegradation. Compared to plant-based fabrics, animal-based materials such as 202 
wool and silk biodegradable slower, but they would be fully biodegraded in the natural 203 
environment after two to four years at the most. Overall, the persistency of synthetic fibers is 204 
hundreds to several thousand times higher than natural products (Fig. 4). Thus, replacing 205 
synthetic fabrics with natural products is the most effective strategy to reduce the impact of 206 
MF on the aquatic environment.  207 

 
 
14 
 
Natural fabrics are expensive and often unaffordable for customers in low-income countries. 208 
Consequently, developing and applying of advanced technologies in natural fabrics 209 
production could increase productivity and reduce this material's cost. These are valuable 210 
solutions to help natural fabrics reach a more comprehensive range of customers. Blending or 211 
surface coating natural fabrics with other materials could be an idea to increase fabric 212 
strength to overcome the current drawback of these materials. Besides, changing to 213 
regenerated cellulosic fabrics (rayon) was an alternative choice for attracting consumers and 214 
more safe for the environment. Rayon is a fabric made from purified cellulose fibers, which 215 
are typically created from wood pulp (Mendes et al., 2021). For example, Tencel®, a type of 216 
rayon, like viscose and modal, was also made from wood pulp or bamboo. As a plant-derived 217 
fiber, Tencel is also biodegradable. Tencel requires a lot less dye than cotton. When 218 
produced, it is also pure white, so no bleaching is necessary, and undyed is always an 219 
excellent alternative to synthetic fabrics (Basit et al., 2019). 220 
Additional research to improve the texture properties and durabilities of natural fabrics can 221 
help them compete with synthetic materials. These can include work to improve the strength 222 
and abrasion resistance, breathability, water resistance, quick-drying properties of natural 223 
fabrics. Increasing consumer awareness about the harmful effects of synthetic fabrics can also 224 
help the effort to phase out synthetic fabrics. 225 
4.2. Future roadmap 226 
The ecological impact of MFs in natural water is an environmental problem of global scale. 227 
Thus, solving this problem is the responsibility of more than just individual households or the 228 
consumers. Indeed, a holistic solution should cover the entire supply chain, starting from the 229 
textile industry and including municipal wastewater treatment, washing machine 230 
manufracturers and the consumers. 231 

 
 
15 
 
The textile industry has a significant role to play in resovling this problem. By limiting the 232 
use of synthetic plastic-based fabrics, the reduction of MFs release can be achieved at the 233 
source. Natural fibers have a very short lifetime in the environment as they are fully 234 
biodegradable (Fig. 4). Although synthetic fabrics are often cheaper and easier to 235 
manufacture than natural fabrics, consumer setiment to support nature-based products is 236 
growing in recent years. Further progress in materials science and engineering is expected to 237 
make natural fabrics more durable and with better texture properties to compete with 238 
synthetic materials. 239 
Large-scale removal of microfibers from the environment is neither technically feasible nor 240 
economically viable, so the focus needs to be on preventing the release of MFs to the 241 
environment at the first place. Less than 50% of the global population is currently served by 242 
WWTPs. Increasing the global coverage of centralised wastewater treatment can significantly 243 
reduce MFs release to the environment. As discussed in section 2.2.2, washing condition can 244 
have some impact on the release of MFs. By reducing washing temperature, detergent 245 
consumption, and abration, the release of MFs to laundry wastewater can be reduced. These 246 
measures can also reduce the energy and chemical footprint of laundry activities. Last but not 247 
least, the use of MFs traps and filters should be encouraged. Although the efficiency of these 248 
devices is low and variable, they are low cost and can be readily implemented.  249 
5. Conclusions 250 
The release of microfibers (MFs) into the aquatic environment primarily from laundry 251 
activities has emerged as a globally significant environmental issue. There are several ways 252 
in which MFs can negatively impact the aquatic ecosystem. MFs can act as carriers of other 253 
contaminats, cause physical damage to digestive systems of aquatic organisms, block the 254 
digestive tract, release toxic chemicals to internal organs, and harbour invasive and noxious 255 
plankton and bacteria in the aquatic ecosystem. Results corroborated from this review show 256

that no single solution can adequately address the problem of MFs in the aquatic 257 
environment. Small devices to capture MFs during household laundry activities are low cost 258 
and simple to use. However, these devices are unreliable and can only achieve low or 259 
moderate MF removal efficiency compared to conventional wastewater treatment, which can 260 
completely intercept and prevent the release of MFs into the aquatic environment. In fact, 261 
effort to increase sanitation coverage to ensure adequate wastewater treatment prior to 262 
environmental discharge is likely to be more cost effective than those small devices for 263 
capturing MFs. This review demonstrates that natural fabrics would entail significantly less 264 
environmental consequences than synthetic materials. The fashion industry can also address 265 
the problem of MFs in the environment by phasing out synthetic fabrics and increase the use 266 
of natural materials. 267
