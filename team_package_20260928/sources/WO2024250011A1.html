The timing and extent of microplastic release can vary depending on factors such as fabric type, washing machine settings, and detergent composition.
  • Agitation/Soak This phase involves the physical agitation of clothes in water, which helps to dislodge dirt, stains, and debris. It is during this stage that significant friction occurs between the fabric fibers, leading to the shedding of microplastics into the water.
  • Detergent Dispersion Detergents contain surfactants and other chemicals that help to lift dirt and grime from clothing and suspend it in the water. The addition of detergent can increase the release of microplastics by further loosening fibers from the fabric.
  • Rinsing In this phase, the washing machine typically drains the soapy water and refills with clean water to rinse away detergent residue and suspended particles. While some microplastics may be flushed out during rinsing, a significant portion may remain suspended in the water due to their buoyancy.
  • Drainage The final phase involves draining the water from the washing machine, carrying any suspended microplastics with it as it exits the machine.

  • Circular-shaped holes have uniform flow characteristics. They provide a straightforward pathway for fluid flow and are less prone to flow restrictions compared to other shapes.   • Square or rectangular holes offer similar advantages to circular holes in terms of simplicity and ease of fabrication. They provide a uniform flow pathway and can facilitate efficient particle capture. Square or rectangular holes may be preferred in applications where space constraints or specific housing designs necessitate a more geometrically regular shape.
  • Triangular Slits can provide sharp edges that promote particle capture and retention.
  • the triangular shape may also create vortices or eddies in the fluid flow, enhancing particle-surface interactions and filtration efficiency.
  • Hexagonal or honeycomb patterns feature interconnected hexagonal-shaped holes arranged in a repeating pattern. These patterns maximize the surface area available for fluid flow while minimizing the amount of material required, resulting in a lightweight and efficient design. Hexagonal or honeycomb patterns can promote uniform flow distribution and enhance particle capture efficiency, making them suitable for high-performance filtration applications.
  • Irregular or random hole patterns can offer advantages in certain applications where enhanced fluid mixing or particle-surface interactions are desired. These patterns create diverse flow pathways and flow velocities, which can increase the likelihood of particle capture and retention. However, irregular or random patterns can be more challenging to fabricate and optimize compared to regular geometric shapes.

Abstract

A highly-efficient, low-cost microfiber trapping device (100, 600, 700, 800, 900, 1000, 1100) used in consumers' homes to reduce microplastic emissions at the point source- laundry. In an example implementation, the device (100, 600, 700, 800, 900, 1000, 1100) tumbles freely throughout the washer. The device is made of up two reusable, hemispherical halves (602a, 602b) which lock into place and hold a porous, disposable inner filter. In a second implementation, the device is attached to the inside of the machine during the washing cycle. The disposable, porous inner filter, such as one generated via electrospinning, has controllable pore sizes ranging from 10 to 100 µm to trap microfibers in a range of lengths. As the washing machine is run, the device captures microfibers as laundry water flows through it. In another example implementation, the device (100, 600, 700, 800, 900, 1000, 1100) has a rigid, outer shell is a rod-like container of adjustable size with a attachment means (830) for the fixation on the drum of the washing machine.

BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION
[0002] Microfibers released from synthetic textiles account for -35% of the microplastic waste entering the oceans. Millions of microfibers are shed in washing machines, which mechanically and chemically agitate clothing as part of the wash process. Once the microfibers are transported from the home to water treatment plants along with the laundry waste water, they inevitably are released into our ecosystem and make their way into the oceans. Marine organisms ingest these particles, often mistaking them for food. As microplastics have been found to leach plastic additives and adsorb contaminants from their surrounding environments (e.g., heavy metals and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons), ingestion of these particles can be toxic. Microplastic ingestion by humans and animals may lead to cell damage and chronic inflammation. It is critical to collect these microfibers before they enter the water waste stream, and as such, collecting microfibers within the washing machine can prevent them from ending up in the oceans.
[0003] There are currently three product categories on the market targeting the collection of microfibers in washing machine wastewater. External filters, like the Filtrol, are installed outside of the washing machine and collect the microfibers before they enter the waste stream. While the Filtrol has high efficiency, the filter is expensive compared to market alternatives and not accessible to everyone due to inconvenient or prohibited installation. Laundry bags are an affordable alternative but have middling efficiency. Laundry bags also have low size specificity and miss smaller fibers. Laundry balls are another affordable alternative, but the products currently on the market also have low efficiency and size specificity. As such, the current competitor landscape is characterized by solutions that can only partially meet the needs of eco-conscious green consumers and there exist glaring gaps in cost-effectiveness, ease of use, and microfiber capture efficiency. [0004] According to aspects herein, a filter device has a hard, semipermeable outer shell with a fine porous inner core designed to filter microfibers from the inside of the washing machine during a laundry cycle. There are several products on the market designed to eliminate microfibers from the laundry machine source; the filter device as disclosed herein has a combination of features not observed in other conventional products.
SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION
[0005] The invention provides a first-in-class, highly-efficient, low-cost microfiber trapping device used in consumers’ homes to reduce microplastic emissions at the point source — laundry. In a first embodiment, the device tumbles freely throughout the washer. The device is made up of two reusable, hemispherical halves which lock into place and hold a porous, disposable inner filter. In a second embodiment, the device is attached to the inside of the machine, during the washing cycle. The outer shell has multiple possible attachment mechanisms, for example, a pin mechanism which interacts with washing machine drainage holes. The disposable, porous inner filter, such as one generated via electrospinning, has controllable pore sizes ranging from 10 to 100 pm to trap microfibers in a range of lengths. DETAILED DESCRIPTION
[0030] During a typical washing machine cycle, microplastics can be released from synthetic fabrics such as polyester, nylon, and acrylic. These microplastics are typically shed in the form of fibers due to the mechanical action of washing, the use of detergents, and the friction between clothes. The timing and extent of microplastic release can vary depending on factors such as fabric type, washing machine settings, and detergent composition.
[0031] Research suggests that the majority of microplastics are released from clothing during the washing phase of the cycle, particularly during agitation. The following is a breakdown of the wash cycle and when microplastics are most likely to become intermixed with the water.
[0032] Agitation/Soak: This phase involves the physical agitation of clothes in water, which helps to dislodge dirt, stains, and debris. It is during this stage that significant friction occurs between the fabric fibers, leading to the shedding of microplastics into the water.
[0033] Detergent Dispersion: Detergents contain surfactants and other chemicals that help to lift dirt and grime from clothing and suspend it in the water. The addition of detergent can increase the release of microplastics by further loosening fibers from the fabric.
[0034] Rinsing: In this phase, the washing machine typically drains the soapy water and refills with clean water to rinse away detergent residue and suspended particles. While some microplastics may be flushed out during rinsing, a significant portion may remain suspended in the water due to their buoyancy.
[0035] Spin Cycle: During the spin cycle, the washing machine's drum rapidly rotates to remove excess water from the clothes by centrifugal force. This intense mechanical action can further dislodge microplastics from the fabric and entrain them in the water.
[0036] Drainage: The final phase involves draining the water from the washing machine, carrying any suspended microplastics with it as it exits the machine.
[0037] A consideration in the filter design are the holes in the outer housing that allow the laundry water to pass through the inner filter. In general, the more surface area exposed, the more filtration possible. Below are considerations given to the various slit geometries, any of which is contemplated herein for use in connection with any filter housing disclosed herein.
[0038] Circular-shaped holes have uniform flow characteristics. They provide a straightforward pathway for fluid flow and are less prone to flow restrictions compared to other shapes. Circular holes can promote efficient particle capture and minimize pressure drop, making them suitable for a wide range of filtration applications.
[0039] Square or rectangular holes offer similar advantages to circular holes in terms of simplicity and ease of fabrication. They provide a uniform flow pathway and can facilitate efficient particle capture. Square or rectangular holes may be preferred in applications where space constraints or specific housing designs necessitate a more geometrically regular shape.
[0040] Triangular Slits: Triangular slits can provide sharp edges that promote particle capture and retention. The triangular shape may also create vortices or eddies in the fluid flow, enhancing particle-surface interactions and filtration efficiency.
[0041] Hexagonal or honeycomb patterns feature interconnected hexagonal-shaped holes arranged in a repeating pattern. These patterns maximize the surface area available for fluid flow while minimizing the amount of material required, resulting in a lightweight and efficient design. Hexagonal or honeycomb patterns can promote uniform flow distribution and enhance particle capture efficiency, making them suitable for high-performance filtration applications.
[0042] Irregular or random hole patterns can offer advantages in certain applications where enhanced fluid mixing or particle-surface interactions are desired. These patterns create diverse flow pathways and flow velocities, which can increase the likelihood of particle capture and retention. However, irregular or random patterns can be more challenging to fabricate and optimize compared to regular geometric shapes.
[0043] While considering water flow, another design constraint for the filters disclosed herein is retaining mechanical durability/strength in the housing shell. Due to the sloshing of water and relatively high centrifugal forces that the filter must endure, while maximizing water flow is important for maximum microparticle capture, the housing must also be able to hold up to water, textiles, and centrifugal forces of the spinning drum of the laundry machine.
[0044] Another consideration is the material or materials of the filters used herein. The following filters and materials can be used in connection with any filter device disclosed herein. An inspiration for the filters herein is sea sponges, which are nature’ s filters and filter feeders. Every day, a sea sponge can filter between two and 20 cubic meters of water per kilogram of bodyweight. [0045] Unlike a soft, squishy kitchen sponge, sea sponges are stiff and strong. They have an amazingly complex skeleton that consists of a spiral hierarchical design of fibers, known as spicules, no larger than a human hair. This intricate multi-layered and cylindrical shape allows water to flow in and out of its body while enduring a high-pressure environment.
[0046] Taking cues from sea sponges, the bio-inspired sponge-like membrane filter herein provides unparalleled convenience and efficiency. Through physical entrapment and adsorption mechanisms, high porosity, high surface area, and open-celled structures, the filter herein achieves targeted, reliable, and effective microplastics capture during the laundry cycle.
[0047] Similar to how the sea sponge’s unique structural pattern is composed of different thicknesses and angles of orientation at specific distances, the inner filter according to some aspects herein is spun to have a gradation of pores (as you go from the outside closer to the core, each layer is a more porous, tighter mesh).
[0048] Another natural inspiration comes from suspension feeders like oysters, which use their gills to retain suspended particles while cilia actively pump water through small openings. This concept influenced the filter’s design within the washing machine drum. By positioning the filter inside the drum, the natural flow of water and the centrifugal force of the spin cycle are harnessed, eliminating the need for external pumps, batteries, or additional energy usage. The devices herein can incorporate inner channels and doors that open and close depending on water flow direction, mimicking the behavior of cilia to further enhance filtration efficiency.
[0049] The filter characteristics or attributes considered herein include geometries, densities, porosities, thicknesses, composition, form factors, and surface modifications. The filter must be suited for the specific high-flux, basic (7-10 pH), temperature range (60°F - 130°F or 45°F - 150°F), and turbulent environment inside the spinning or agitating drum of the washing machine. Examples of materials for the filter include: Cellulose acetate/acetone electrospun membrane, cellulose acetate/acetone aerogels, open-cell polyurethane-based foam (35 & 45 PPI), non-woven fine filter wool, polyurethane sponge, cellulose sponge, silicone sponge, pleated cellulose membrane filter (3, 8, 11, 20-25pm), unpleated cellulose membrane filter (3, 8, 11, 20-25pm), pleated coarse apertured 100% viscose spunlace nonwoven, unpleated coarse apertured 100% viscose spunlace nonwoven, and stainless steel mesh (100 & 100 micron).
[0050] Suitable surface modifications/adsorbents include: Fe3O4 + PDMS coating, which repels water while attracting nonpolar plastics (i.e., polypropylene + polyethylene), activated carbon to increase surface area, and amine-functionalized: precedent shown to remove lead and heavy metals from water.
[0051] Surprising results from testing the influence of orientation, shape, length, thickness, and porosity on capture efficiency in real-world environment include the following observations: 30 PPI vs 45 PPI only marginally different in pre-milled microfiber capture efficiency; aerogels created by treating 2D cellulose acetate/acetone electrospun mats with sodium borohydride and vacuum drying exhibit similar capture efficiency, but are extremely fragile and exhibited poor mechanical strength; better performing trials favored 4.5” length filters over 9” length; most of the trials that worked well only had 2 layers in thickness; in most trials, the inside of the filter (facing the drum of the wall) exhibits higher visual indication of capture than the outside; areas of filter medium compressed by elastic band or folded exhibit increased visual indication; gradation, i.e., layering non-woven fine filter wool with stainless steel mesh plate (installed flush against surface of the drum) helps reduce microfibers lost due to high centrifugal force during the rinse and spin cycles; lifespan tests indicate that the filters can last at least 10 cycles without significant resistance; however, there is potential in back-flushing during the wash cycle. The microfibers are captured on the surface of the pleated filter in a moderately reversible way, and aspects of the present disclosure leverage the centrifugal force and a pulse to knock them off again and move the microfibers to a separate compartment where they get stored. Filters could maintain longer life over longer periods of time, preventing clogging and fouling.
[0052] Gradient-layered filters of specific mesh sizes (macroparticles [250 pm], human hair [100 pm], pet hair, [50 pm], microplastics [10 pm]) are also contemplated.
[0053] Some aspects include a multi-stage filter having at least an inner filter and a second stage filter. The inner filter is designed with a large surface area to maximize contact with the water flow. This can be achieved, for example, by using a filter material that can be folded or pleated to increase the effective surface area without significantly increasing the size of the filter housing. [0054] The filter device is installed in a location where the water most often passes. Horizontal and vertical orientations of the filter are contemplated; however, it has been found that horizontally orienting the filter at the bottom of a top loader and as far as back as possible in a front loader produces optimum filtration.
[0055] The filter design directs the flow of water to the filter media by spanning the width of the drum and using walls to guide the water into the media. [0056] The filter retains the microplastics from the fast-spinning and agitation forces during its second stage of filtration via its layered design. The nonwoven mat captures the microfibers during the initial sloshing of water in the machine. After this capture, the microfibers are pushed out of the nonwoven mat and onto a fine mesh that holds and retains the fibers.
[0057] Via gradation/layered membrane filter design: Implementing a multi-stage filtration system where water passes through multiple layers of filters with progressively smaller pore sizes can effectively capture microplastics. This approach distributes the force exerted by the spinning drum across multiple filters.
